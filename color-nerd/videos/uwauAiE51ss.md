## Claim ledger

1. Donahue introduces Amy Sawyer as an artist worth recovering from the margins of standard color-theory history.
2. He situates her practice at the turn of the twentieth century and emphasizes that her work combined academic training with newer Impressionist/Post-Impressionist approaches to color and decorative composition.
3. He shows both painting and mixed/decorative work, then cites contemporary reception that praised the richness/harmony of her color while simultaneously diminishing the work as decoration.
4. Sawyer's relevance is not only stylistic: Donahue identifies her as a color theorist who later designed and patented a wheel/system of her own.
5. The primary labels in that system were **primrose, rose, and turquoise** rather than the canonical schoolbook red, yellow, blue.
6. Donahue's point is that these positions lie much closer to the useful subtractive yellow-magenta-cyan family than ordinary RYB terminology suggests.
7. Sawyer therefore becomes evidence against a tidy history in which everyone before modern printing simply accepted one RYB orthodoxy.

## Historical details to preserve

- Amy Sawyer: 1863–1945.
- Painter, illustrator, writer/playwright, associated with Ditchling, Sussex.
- Her color-system work followed her active painting career.
- The wheel/patent is dated around 1911 in the source trail used for these notes.

## Qualification

The exact correspondence `primrose/rose/turquoise → Y/M/C` should remain approximate. Historical pigment/color names do not uniquely identify modern colorimetric coordinates, and a patent diagram is not evidence that three ideal pigments behaved exactly like process inks.

## Albers relevance

Donahue's historical recovery is methodologically useful: compare actual artifacts and exercises, not a retrospective textbook genealogy. For Albers, that means preserving the specific Color-aid/material editions and student exercises rather than reducing his work to whatever later “Bauhaus color wheel” story is most familiar.
