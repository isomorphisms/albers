## Claim ledger

1. Donahue starts with the familiar trichromatic picture: varying relative activity among three receptor classes gives a circuit of chromatic directions.
2. He asks how that picture changes for an observer with a fourth, UV-sensitive receptor class.
3. Because humans cannot display or imagine the UV-associated percept directly, he uses an arbitrary visual placeholder for it. The placeholder is explicitly symbolic.
4. With four receptor vertices, there are four three-vertex subsets. Donahue draws these as four triangular hue circuits: the ordinary human-visible triplet plus three circuits containing the UV vertex.
5. Those four triangular faces assemble naturally as the faces of a tetrahedron.
6. His key correction is that adding a fourth primary/receptor does not merely turn a color wheel into a flat square. The independent mixture proportions require an additional dimension.
7. He then adds the analogue of lightness/tint/shade variation. If the chromatic proportions themselves occupy a three-dimensional tetrahedral domain, adding an independent achromatic/intensity dimension leads to a four-dimensional full appearance model in the toy picture.

## Geometry worth preserving

For normalized receptor proportions, `n` independent receptor channels live on an `(n-1)`-dimensional simplex before an overall intensity coordinate is restored:

- three channels → triangular/simplex chromaticity domain;
- four channels → tetrahedral/simplex chromaticity domain.

Donahue presents this visually rather than as simplex terminology, but that is the clean mathematical residue.

## Teaching-model caveat

A receptor simplex is not by itself a complete perceptual hue space. Neural opponency, adaptation, receptor noise, oil droplets in many birds, species variation, and nonlinearity all matter. The tetrahedron is a useful response-coordinate model, not a claim that subjective bird color experience literally has Euclidean tetrahedral geometry.

## Albers relevance

The video reinforces a rule for the repository: never name a geometry without stating what has been quotiented or normalized away. A stimulus space, receptor-response space, appearance space, and contextual-equivalence space may all have different geometries even when people casually call each of them “color space.”
