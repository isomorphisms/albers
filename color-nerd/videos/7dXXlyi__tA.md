## Main thread

Donahue uses audience responses to hundreds of short color lessons to identify recurring failures in ordinary color teaching. His target is the habit of teaching a clean rule first and treating contradictory paint behavior as student error.

Traditional complement pairs are one example: geometric opposition on a classroom wheel does not guarantee that two actual pigments will mix to an achromatic result. He also distinguishes domain-specific conventions from universal claims about color.

## Pigment mixing

The most important technical section concerns curved mixture paths. Paint mixtures do not generally travel through appearance space according to a single straight-line rule. Different starting hues can bow toward or away from neutral and intermediate hues can behave unexpectedly.

Donahue therefore treats simple subtractive mixing as a teaching approximation. He points to David Briggs's account of paint mixing as combining subtractive effects with averaging, Harold Küppers's term `integrated mixing`, and Kubelka–Munk theory as a more rigorous physical treatment.

## Color Disc

His Color Disc is an attempt to make a beginner-facing map that is less misleading than the standard RYB wheel. It uses CIECAM02-derived hue organization, gives the achromatic region finite area, and is intended to accommodate curved mixture behavior rather than promising that every mixture follows a straight chord.

## Named references

David Briggs, Bruce MacEvoy, Harold Küppers, Johannes Itten, Chevreul, CIECAM02, and practical pigment-plotting tools all appear in the surrounding discussion.

## Albers connection

The methodological overlap is strong: a diagram is a model to test against appearance, not a truth that observation must obey. Keep material mixing, coordinate geometry, and perceptual equivalence as separate layers.
