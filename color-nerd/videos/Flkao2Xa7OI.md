## Argument in sequence

1. Donahue rejects the feeling that a designer is failing when complementary, triadic, analogous, or split-complement recipes produce disappointing palettes. His target is the recipe, not the viewer's judgment.
2. He builds a perceptual color-space picture from opponent relationships rather than starting with the classroom RYB wheel. Hue is angular position, chroma is displacement from neutral, and lightness supplies the third direction.
3. He emphasizes that colors sharing a hue angle may be named very differently because lightness and chroma matter. Brown and orange are his memorable example.
4. Traditional harmony categories are reframed as special cases of two more general choices: **how many hue regions are being used, and how much hue contrast separates them?**
5. Monochromatic, analogous, complementary, triadic, and tetradic schemes therefore become a sparse set of presets in a much larger design space rather than natural laws.
6. He points out that a strongly restricted hue range can make contextual/opponent effects conspicuous: a neutral embedded in a strongly biased field may appear shifted toward the absent opponent.
7. For generating original palettes, he proposes an exercise that deliberately avoids equal spacing. Start from a key color, make several candidate palettes, vary chroma levels, and avoid repeating the same angular interval between successive hues.
8. The intended result is hierarchy and rhythm without outsourcing the palette to a wheel template.

## Practical rules from the exercise

- Choose an explicit key color rather than an abstract harmony name.
- Vary chroma so every color is not competing at the same perceptual strength.
- Use unequal hue intervals instead of automatically constructing regular polygons on a wheel.
- Make several drafts; the method is exploratory rather than a theorem that one arrangement must be harmonious.
- Know approximately where the actual pigments or display colors occupy the space instead of trusting their names.

## Important conceptual residue

The most useful generalization is that named harmony schemes are coordinates inside a larger search problem. That dovetails with Donahue's later color-harmony lecture: palette behavior depends on lightness and chroma structure, communication, and legibility, not merely angular hue relations.

## Albers relevance

This is strongly Albers-compatible in method. The useful object is not an isolated hue name but a relation among patches with controlled differences. It suggests that an Albers fixture generator should vary hue interval, chroma hierarchy, and lightness hierarchy independently rather than organizing experiments around inherited “complementary/triadic” labels.
