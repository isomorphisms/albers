# Josef Albers on color and the square

This file records primary-source leads and short summaries rather than reproducing Albers's copyrighted prose.

## `Interaction of Color` (1963)

Foundation overview:

https://www.albersfoundation.org/alberses/teaching/interaction-of-color

The opening claim is that a color is almost never perceived simply as its physical value. Context continually changes appearance, so Albers begins from experiments rather than from a fixed color system. The Foundation describes the course as repeated exercises in what colors do to one another when placed together.

Useful computational reading: the perceived result is not a function of an isolated color coordinate alone; neighboring conditions are part of the input.

## `On my Homage to the Square`

The Foundation archive lists a typescript under this title, circa 1954:

https://www.albersfoundation.org/resources/archives/josef-anni-albers-papers/josef-albers-papers/writings/various-writings

A Toledo Museum label quotes Albers's later published description of the series: the different palettes create different "climates," and the choice and order of colors is aimed at mutual interaction.

https://emuseum.toledomuseum.org/objects/54798/homage-to-the-square--white-setting

The important point is not merely that the squares contain different RGB-like values. The ordering and neighborhood relations are part of the phenomenon being investigated.

## 1968 oral-history interview

Full transcript, Archives of American Art:

https://www.aaa.si.edu/collections/interviews/oral-history-interview-josef-albers-11847

Albers discusses colors changing one another in lightness/intensity and transparency/opacity, explicitly asking how they change each other "in all different directions." He also stresses changing illumination, neighboring conditions, and the observer's changing state.

He explains his preference for the square partly because its orientation is visually explicit: unlike a circle, a square shows whether it is sitting on one of its sides. He presents the repeated square format as a stable form within which many color performances can be investigated.

This is directly useful for a deformation model: hold much of the geometry fixed, move through color choices, and observe a context-dependent perceptual response.

## SFMOMA audio transcript: `Homage to the Square: Starting` (1968)

https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/76.22/

The page includes Albers's recorded voice. He says that he is interested in the interaction of color—how one color changes another—and that he wants his colors to "act." He also distinguishes the physical color in front of us from the perceptual or psychic effect.

The same transcript explains why the serial form matters: one form can sustain hundreds or thousands of performances, so the geometry can remain nearly fixed while the color relations vary.

## Working interpretation for this repository

Do not prematurely turn these observations into a finished mathematical model. For now they support several testable facts:

- perception depends on a color together with context, not the color coordinate alone;
- nearby changes can have strongly direction-dependent perceptual effects;
- a stable geometric carrier can be used to study a family of color deformations;
- changing the surround can change the appearance of a physically unchanged patch;
- repeated variants are evidence, not redundant decoration.
