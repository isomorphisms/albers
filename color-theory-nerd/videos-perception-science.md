# Color Nerd — perception, light, and color-science videos

Peter Donahue's strongest short videos often begin with a physical or spatial demonstration rather than a definition. The notes below preserve the teaching point while flagging places where the short uses a simplified model.

## Chroma, saturation, lightness, brightness

### How Chroma and Saturation Are Different

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/IlpD9DXiH-c
- Date: 2023-03-29

Donahue separates two quantities that everyday art/software vocabulary often treats as synonyms.

- **Chroma**: how different a color is from a neutral of comparable lightness; visualize it as distance outward from a neutral axis.
- **Saturation**: colorfulness in proportion to brightness; visualize equal-saturation paths as rays associated with the black point rather than constant distance from gray.

The point is not merely terminology. Two colors can have the same chroma but different saturation, or the same saturation but different chroma. A rectangular software picker often warps this geometry for computational convenience.

### Saturation vs. Chroma — interactive companion

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/xhYNtMvWbZ4
- Interactive: https://petertdonahue.com/Saturation-vs-Chroma.html

Expands the previous short into a hue-plane visualization. Equal-chroma and equal-saturation contours behave differently as lightness changes. Donahue contrasts perceptual organization with the convenient but distorted geometry of common computer color pickers and references DIN 6164 and OKLCH.

### How Are Lightness and Brightness Different?

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/5qbtjJe1V2o
- Date: 2023-04-03

A painted-cube illusion demonstrates the contextual nature of lightness judgments. A patch can appear plausibly light-colored in one illumination context, then appear almost self-luminous when the surrounding shadow cues are manipulated.

Donahue frames **lightness** as a relative judgment tied to the apparent illumination and **brightness** as a more direct judgment of apparent emitted/reflected intensity. The physical demonstration is more important than treating the few-second verbal definition as a full CIE glossary entry.

## Warm/cool and color temperature

### Is Green WARM or COOL?

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/WQYLfwa2lAI
- Date: 2025-06-17

Donahue sketches idealized spectral-reflectance profiles. A spectrum increasingly weighted toward long wavelengths gives the family we normally call warm; one increasingly weighted toward short wavelengths gives the cool family. A middle-peaked green does not naturally lie on either monotonic slope, and purple combines the two ends.

His teaching conclusion: **green and purple are not intrinsically warm or cool in the same sense as red/orange versus cyan/blue; they can be shifted toward a warmer or cooler side.** This is a useful corrective to palette folklore, though the exact mapping from spectral distribution to perceived temperature is more complicated than the idealized curves.

### Warm / Cool Colors — Color Temperature Is Not Just Hue

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/XmQ9O4efPrQ
- Date: 2025-06-05

Temperature is not simply moving clockwise or counterclockwise on a hue wheel. Donahue models warming or cooling an entire palette as a **systematic change in both hue and saturation/chroma**. A warmer gamut can make reds/oranges more vivid while pushing blues and turquoises toward gray; greens can become yellower and duller; indigos can move toward magenta while losing saturation.

The important painter's insight is global: a change in illumination or spectral bias moves the *whole palette* in a structured way rather than attaching a fixed warm/cool label to each swatch.

### Color Temperature = Spectral Bias

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/M3py_iSpuyA

A compact physical account of color temperature as the way an illuminant favors regions of the visible spectrum. Donahue connects this to outdoor painting, where direct illumination and blue-scattered skylight can give lit and shadow regions different spectral biases.

Pair with *Warm Light, Cool Shadow... Or Vice Versa?* in [`videos-long-form.md`](videos-long-form.md) and the Drawing America temperature course.

## Additive/subtractive relations and physical light

### Magical Color Theory — RGB Shadows

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/q6l2-l9e50o
- Date: 2024-03-26

Three red, green, and blue flashlights overlap to make approximately white light. An object placed in the beams casts cyan, magenta, and yellow shadows because each shadow removes one of the three illumination components:

- remove red → green + blue = cyan;
- remove green → red + blue = magenta;
- remove blue → red + green = yellow.

It is a particularly effective physical demonstration of why RGB and CMY are complementary descriptions of additive and subtractive relationships rather than unrelated computer-versus-paint conventions.

### Iridescence Is Crazy

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/hCGv73cA0yA
- Date: 2025-01-27

Uses an iridescent transparent sheet and colored/white illumination to demonstrate angle-dependent thin-film interference. Different wavelength bands are reinforced or canceled in reflection and transmission as the angle changes. The reflected color and transmitted/shadow color therefore change together in complementary-looking ways.

This is a good example of Donahue's teaching style: start with an object behaving "impossibly," then resolve it spectrally.

### Iridescent Color Math

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/PJUFzgmtOBI

Continues the iridescent-material theme and emphasizes that an optical material can involve additive and subtractive-looking effects at once. Useful as an antidote to the classroom habit of forcing every color phenomenon into one of two perfectly separate boxes called additive and subtractive mixing.

## Human cone responses and unusual gamuts

### Newly Discovered Color — Olo

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/MLG5jjPUwTA
- Date: 2025-05-02

Donahue explains the 2025 "olo" result by plotting cone-response ratios rather than pretending researchers discovered a previously unknown wavelength. The experiment mapped individual retinal cone cells and used tightly targeted 488 nm laser stimulation to favor M-cone responses while suppressing the L- and S-cone activation that normally accompanies that wavelength.

His key distinction is good: **the novelty is an otherwise inaccessible cone-response ratio, not a new part of the electromagnetic spectrum.** He also explains why imperfect spatial isolation of neighboring cones limits how extreme the induced experience can be.

## Tetrachromacy and bird-color thought experiments

### Birds See Different Complementary Colors Than Us!

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/RVPEXrHOiOI
- Date: 2025-05-15

Donahue builds a deliberately simplified four-channel thought experiment: if a visual system has an additional UV-sensitive cone class, human complement pairs no longer exhaust the ways channels can divide into complementary groups. He invents memorable labels such as "ultra cyan" and "gruv" to reason about 1+3 and 2+2 channel combinations.

The conceptual point is worthwhile: **complementarity depends on the observer's visual system, not only on external wavelengths.**

Caveat: real avian vision varies by species and includes cone sensitivities, oil droplets, neural opponent processing, and ecological adaptation. Treat the named "bird colors" in the short as a teaching construction, not a standardized zoological color vocabulary.

### Bird Hue Circuits & Tetrahedra

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/_Uq1vb5UtoM

Extends the tetrachromat thought experiment into a tetrahedral representation. Four receptor channels require a different geometry from the triangle commonly used for trichromatic chromaticity.

Again, the useful lesson is geometric rather than literal: adding an independent receptor dimension changes the space of possible response ratios and therefore changes what "opposite," "between," and "same hue" can mean.

## Historical model used to explain perception

### Gerritsen's Spectral Perception Wheel

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/49k6jJOH3Pc

Donahue discusses Frans Gerritsen's 1970s color system, which uses transparent/acetate overlays to connect cyan, magenta, and yellow spectral absorptions with the three human cone-response classes. It is a historical model he likes because the user can physically manipulate the relationship between stimulus and perception rather than merely memorize a wheel.

See also the longer Gerritsen video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zOs0C-PvRmY

## Why these belong beside Albers

Albers's great pedagogical fact is that color appearance is relational. Donahue's perception videos supply mechanisms and modern vocabulary for some of the same instability:

- illumination changes appearance;
- surrounding context changes lightness/brightness judgments;
- the same nominal hue can occupy very different chroma/lightness positions;
- the observer's visual system constrains complementarity;
- a flat color chip does not encode its spectral composition or behavior in mixture.

The two approaches should not be collapsed into one another. Albers's exercises are phenomenological demonstrations; Donahue often asks for a physical, physiological, or colorimetric explanation after the perceptual surprise.
