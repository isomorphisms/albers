# Color Nerd — color history, wheels, and ordering systems

Donahue's historical videos are not side trivia. They support a central argument: many rules now taught as timeless "color theory" are artifacts of particular printing technologies, pigments, industries, national traditions, or classroom diagrams.

## Color Wheel Wednesday

### Episode 1 — Ostwald hue circle

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/_3-s2gM9MIM
- Date: 2026-03-11

Donahue opens Wilhelm Ostwald's *Color Primer* to the 24-hue circle and stresses what the wheel was actually for. It is a **psychological/perceptual ordering system**, not an RYB pigment recipe. Yellow sits opposite an ultramarine-like blue. He traces its adoption through British school materials, Winsor & Newton/Reeves products, Egbert Jacobson in the United States, and even Higgins inks.

His explanation for the system's decline is instructive: it was a closed standard. New, more vivid colorants could become awkward to place, while later systems were more adaptable.

## RYB, CMY, and the history of "primaries"

### RYB vs. CMY Color Wheels

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/LfgswanxffM
- Date: 2025-07-18

Donahue compares a mid-century Met/Junior Museum RYB teaching wheel with a 1988 Process Color Selector based on cyan, magenta, and yellow. He demonstrates the practical problem with treating ordinary "red, yellow, blue" swatches as ideal subtractive primaries: red plus a yellow may remain strongly red, while a red plus cyan-leaning blue can collapse toward near-black instead of producing a brilliant purple.

The CMY selector makes mixture percentages explicit and spans useful oranges, greens, and purples much more predictably. The larger lesson is that **which pigments and proportions you use matters more than the names of three supposedly primary categories.**

### Moses Harris, 1769 — where equal RYB spacing came from

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bAIWcrBN6CY

Donahue argues that Moses Harris was crucial in normalizing equal 120-degree spacing of red, yellow, and blue. Earlier diagrams by Newton, Boutet, and Schiffermüller did not simply hand us the modern school wheel.

The irony is that Harris's own practical wheel needed a fourth pigment, carmine lake, to make a vivid red-violet. Donahue treats this as evidence that later pedagogy inherited the clean diagram more enthusiastically than the messy material facts.

### Amy Sawyer — forgotten CMY theorist

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/uwauAiE51ss

A recovery of Amy Sawyer's early twentieth-century color work. Donahue highlights a subtractive-primary arrangement described with names roughly corresponding to yellow, magenta, and cyan (primrose, rose, turquoise) well before CMY became ordinary graphic-arts vocabulary.

The historical point is not that modern CMY suddenly appeared in one invention, but that alternatives to canonical RYB existed and were repeatedly forgotten.

### Oldest color wheel with "magenta"

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/Xo4VcaEikB0

Tracks when the word *magenta* begins appearing in a primary-color role. Donahue points to Carl Gustav Zander around 1907, while noting older systems used labels such as pink, crimson, or purpur for related positions. Color-history arguments can therefore be obscured if modern names are projected backward onto older diagrams.

## Hybrid perceptual / physiological systems

### Elizabeth Hoppin Lewis, 1931 — trichromacy plus opponent structure

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sv2lUbd8gZ8

Donahue presents a little-known wheel associated with Elizabeth Hoppin Lewis that tries to accommodate both trichromatic RGB ideas and opponent red/green, yellow/blue organization. He connects the scheme to Christine Ladd-Franklin's color-vision theory.

The broader value is methodological: historical color diagrams sometimes contain sophisticated attempts to reconcile physiology and appearance that disappear from simplified textbook genealogies.

### Frans Gerritsen — spectral perception wheel

- Short: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/49k6jJOH3Pc
- Longer discussion: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zOs0C-PvRmY

Gerritsen's 1970s work uses transparent overlays to relate CMY absorptions to trichromatic cone responses. Donahue likes the model because it lets the viewer manipulate how physical filtering and visual response fit together.

## Systems that order color rather than merely mix paint

### Ostwald 24-hue system

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/_3-s2gM9MIM

See above. Important distinction: **color ordering is not the same task as choosing a minimal set of mixing primaries.**

### Munsell — five principal hues and a 3D solid

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=06ul1hBUjaE

The title asks why a wheel would have five primary colors. The answer opens the distinction between a perceptual notation system and a pigment-mixing theory. Munsell organizes hue around a circle while keeping value and chroma as independent dimensions, producing an irregular three-dimensional solid rather than pretending all hues have equal available chroma at every lightness.

### What Japan Did With Munsell That the West Didn't

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IkMsZ5YFEyo

A case study in the cultural afterlife of a color-order system. Donahue uses Japanese engagement with Munsell to show that the same model can be adapted and taught differently across design cultures.

### Cheskin Color Wheel, 1953

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/HNlFFJowKRk

Louis Cheskin's transparent-overlay wheel is interesting to Donahue because the overlay mechanism lets a two-dimensional diagram represent more than one relation at a time. He connects the period to Faber Birren's tint/shade/tone vocabulary and the transition from inherited RYB language toward more CMY-aware practice.

## Spectral and naming history

### What Happened to Indigo?

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/bg_5p6oTaUU

Donahue follows the changing division of the visible spectrum rather than treating Newton's seven labels as fixed natural joints. In nineteenth-century usage, what Newton called blue and indigo do not map cleanly onto contemporary everyday categories; von Bezold's 1874 treatment helps mark the decline of indigo as a separate spectral division.

This is a useful warning for any attempt to assign exact modern RGB samples to historical words.

### Webster / ISCC color definitions

- Videos:
  - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/e33WEqkaPPQ
  - https://www.youtube.com/shorts/v4wCvksrR2M

Donahue explores the twentieth-century attempt to make color names systematic enough for dictionaries, science, trade, and standards work. He discusses figures including I. H. Godlove and the ISCC/NBS naming effort.

His own current website continues this interest with an interactive ISCC-NBS centroid picker: https://petertdonahue.com/

### Web-safe colors

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/B1tfImuPTcA

A much newer historical system: the 216-color web-safe palette. Six permitted channel values produced 6³ = 216 combinations intended to survive limited display hardware and browser/platform differences. Donahue treats it as another example of a color system whose geometry makes sense only when its technological constraint is remembered.

## Artists, pigments, and historical materials

### Caravaggio's copper-resinate greens

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/RezeYJFTacs

A historical pigment/material example rather than a wheel. Donahue discusses transparent copper-resinate glazing over verdigris and the spectral behavior that can create striking greens. The value for this repository is that historical "color theory" also lives in material recipes and workshop practice, not only diagrams.

### Famous Palettes course

- Course: https://drawingamerica.com/courses/famous-palettes-with-peter-donahue-caravaggio/

Four lecture/painting-demo pairs on Caravaggio, Turner, Mary Cassatt, and Munsell. This is Donahue at his most explicitly material-historical: what painters actually had, how the palette constrained the resulting gamut, and how optical effects emerged from those substances.

## A useful taxonomy of the wheels

When reading Donahue, do not ask only "is this wheel correct?" Ask **correct for what?**

A wheel may be trying to represent:

1. perceptual hue ordering;
2. pigment mixing;
3. additive light mixing;
4. physiological receptor/opponent relations;
5. harmony conventions;
6. a commercial palette or standard;
7. color naming;
8. a historical teaching practice.

Many of the fights Donahue stages are cases where a diagram built for one purpose was later presented as if it solved all eight.
