# Color Wheel Wednesday — 2026 working index

Peter Donahue started a recurring **Color Wheel Wednesday** series in 2026, using one historical or commercial wheel at a time to ask what the diagram is actually designed to represent and whether it succeeds.

This is a working index. Episode numbers are included only where they were recoverable from Donahue's own video descriptions or transcript-derived notes.

## Episode 1 — Ostwald hue circle

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/shorts/_3-s2gM9MIM
- Date: 2026-03-11

Donahue goes to a 1930 edition of Wilhelm Ostwald's *Color Primer* and shows the 24-hue Ostwald circle. The essential correction is that it is **not an RYB pigment-mixing wheel**. It is a psychological/perceptual ordering system.

He traces how widely the system spread through British schools, Winsor & Newton/Reeves teaching materials, Egbert Jacobson in the United States, and later commercial inks. He argues that a major weakness was its status as a closed standard: more vivid new materials did not always have an obvious place in the existing system.

## Episode 5 — The Color Wheel that Never Had a Chance

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BJx_7QkhnVA
- Published: 2026-04-08

Donahue calls the **Modern Color Chart (1931)** one of his favorite wheels. The history is a collaboration between two women — a scientist and an artist — who produced an ambitious modern chart just before the surrounding field moved on and rendered it effectively obsolete.

The episode matters for Donahue's historical method: judge a diagram by the problem its makers were solving and the scientific/artistic context available to them, not only by whether its geometry matches a contemporary standard.

## Episode 9 — The Only Color Wheel I Use

- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EACRRXynk1w
- Published: 2026-05-06

This is unusually useful because Donahue finally answers the practical question: **after criticizing so many wheels, what does he actually use in the studio?**

He tests the familiar commercial Color Wheel Company wheel, then points to **Bruce MacEvoy's watercolor pigment wheel**. MacEvoy plots roughly eighty watercolor pigments using **CIECAM02**, making the chart a map of actual pigments in a perceptual color-appearance model rather than a generic twelve-wedge RYB prescription.

Donahue explicitly gives one limitation: MacEvoy's chart is watercolor-specific. The lesson is therefore not "this is the one true wheel." It is closer to:

> Use a map whose underlying measurements and medium match the task you are actually doing.

That is one of the cleanest statements of his overall philosophy.

## Series-level observation

The format turns Donahue's collection of color charts and historical books into a repeated experiment:

1. identify the wheel;
2. identify its date and makers;
3. determine its intended job;
4. inspect what it treats as primary/opposite/equal;
5. compare those claims with actual pigment behavior, perceptual organization, or later color science;
6. explain why the system survived, disappeared, or migrated into a particular craft/industry.

This is a particularly good series for the Albers repository because it refuses to equate **a color system** with **color itself**.
