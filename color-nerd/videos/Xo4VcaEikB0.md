## Historical question

Donahue is not asking when a magenta-like subtractive primary first existed physically. He is asking when the **word “magenta”** became attached to that position in a color-wheel/printing theory.

## Claim ledger

1. He presents a 1907 diagram as the earliest wheel he has found that explicitly uses the word *magenta* for the subtractive-primary position.
2. The color name itself is older, originating in the nineteenth-century synthetic-dye era rather than in modern process printing.
3. Before terminology stabilized, authors recognized a similar required subtractive hue but described it with inconsistent words: forms of crimson, pink, purple/purpur, and other dye names.
4. Donahue points to Carl Gustav Zander as an important step in standardizing *magenta* for the printing/color-system role, with an earlier 1904 patent usage preceding the wheel he displays.
5. He uses William Benson's 1868 terminology as evidence that the physical/structural idea can precede the modern vocabulary: Benson's “seagreen, pink, yellow” arrangement is recognizably close to what would later be called cyan, magenta, yellow.
6. The result is a warning about historical reading: absence of a modern word does not imply absence of the corresponding color-system idea.

## Distinctions to retain

- discovery/use of a dye called magenta;
- recognition of a useful subtractive-primary hue;
- adoption of the word *magenta* as a standardized technical label;
- appearance of that label in a wheel/printing diagram.

These are different historical events.
