## Objects compared

Donahue puts two physical teaching/design artifacts side by side:

- a mid-century Metropolitan Museum / Junior Museum style wheel organized around red-yellow-blue claims;
- a late-1980s Process Color Selector organized around cyan-magenta-yellow percentages.

The point is not merely historical taste. He tests what each diagram predicts about subtractive mixture.

## Claim ledger

1. The older RYB wheel is visually clean and pedagogically familiar, but it asserts that its named red, yellow, and blue behave as three effective subtractive primaries.
2. Donahue tests the red-plus-yellow claim and finds that the represented mixture remains strongly red rather than landing at a satisfying orange midpoint.
3. Yellow plus the selected blue gives a more plausible green; one success does not validate the whole three-primary geometry.
4. Red plus a cyan-leaning blue becomes very dark rather than a vivid purple because their spectral effects suppress much of the light needed for a bright mixture.
5. The process-color wheel treats mixture as both **which colorants** and **how much of each**, exposing percentage as part of the state rather than pretending named primaries alone determine the answer.
6. CMY is presented as a much more useful subtractive basis for process-style color mixing than generic RYB.
7. Donahue's larger complaint is therefore empirical: a wheel should be judged by the job it claims to do. A beautiful hue-ordering diagram is not automatically a predictive pigment-mixture model.

## What not to overgeneralize

CMY process printing is itself an engineered system with particular inks, substrates, dot structure, black generation, and device profiles. Real artist pigments remain materially idiosyncratic. “CMY is better than RYB” here means a better subtractive-primary model, not that three ideal coordinates completely predict arbitrary paint mixtures.

## Albers relevance

Albers's method benefits from exactly this discipline: separate **the arrangement of colors on a diagram** from **the behavior of actual samples**. If a contextual experiment uses printed, painted, emitted, or Color-aid samples, the material/reproduction path belongs in the fixture provenance rather than being silently inferred from a wheel position.
