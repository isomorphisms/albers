# Initial reference set

These are useful works to inspect while developing the color/perception model. They are **references, not reusable image assets**, unless a later rights check establishes otherwise.

## `Homage to the Square: White Setting` — 1959

Toledo Museum of Art:

https://emuseum.toledomuseum.org/objects/54798/homage-to-the-square--white-setting

Why keep it: the museum page pairs the painting with Albers's description of different palettes as different climates and of color order as reciprocal interaction. It is a clean example for asking how fixed geometry plus changed color values alters perceived depth and relation.

Rights: image is downloadable, but no open license covering the underlying Albers artwork is established here.

## `Homage to the Square: Silent Hall` — 1961

MoMA:

https://www.moma.org/collection/works/79438

Why keep it: MoMA explicitly connects the work to Albers's claim that the colors are intended to influence and change one another back and forth.

Rights: copyrighted by the Josef and Anni Albers Foundation / Artists Rights Society.

## `Homage to the Square: Starting` — 1968

SFMOMA:

https://www.sfmoma.org/artwork/76.22/

Why keep it: the page includes Albers's own recorded explanation of the series. He describes color as active and relational, and distinguishes physical fact from perceptual effect.

Rights: copyrighted; use the page and transcript as research sources rather than copying the image.

## `Homage to the Square—Insert` — 1959

Smithsonian American Art Museum:

https://americanart.si.edu/artwork/homage-square-insert-187

Smithsonian collection record:

https://www.si.edu/object/saam_1969.47.51

Why keep it: the museum description points out that the central square may appear to recede or project depending on viewing conditions. That is a direct example of fixed pigment and geometry producing a non-fixed perceptual spatial effect.

Rights: Smithsonian record says `Usage Conditions Apply`.

## `On Tideland` — 1947–1955

Smithsonian American Art Museum:

https://americanart.si.edu/artwork/tideland-205

Why keep it: the museum describes the same gray taking on different appearances beside ochre and pinkish orange, and connects the work with Albers's interest in the discrepancy between physical fact and visual effect. It is particularly useful because the phenomenon is not confined to the nested-square format.

Rights: treat as copyrighted unless separately cleared.

## `Homage to the Square: Young` — 1951–52

Metropolitan Museum of Art:

https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/488725

Why keep it: early *Homage* example and useful comparison point for the long deformation/family of variants.

Rights: the Met record explicitly gives an Artists Rights Society copyright notice and does not make the image available as a public-domain Open Access asset.

## Next useful search

Rather than accumulating hundreds of square images, look for small groups where one variable is controlled:

- nearly identical geometry with one color changed;
- the same or nearly the same physical color embedded in different surrounds;
- examples Albers himself discussed in `Interaction of Color`;
- documented pigment/paint annotations from the backs of paintings;
- student exercises for which reuse rights are clearer than for Albers's paintings.

Those groups can become executable perceptual/deformation fixtures later without pretending that one RGB triple has an intrinsic appearance independent of context.
