## Main argument

Donahue rejects the common idea that harmony can be generated mechanically by taking complementary, triadic, or tetradic intervals from a hue wheel. Equal angular hue spacing says little by itself about perceptual balance, communication, mood, accessibility, or legibility.

One reason is that hue planes do not have identical geometry. Different hues reach their available chroma at different lightnesses and with different gamut limits; the shape also changes with medium and display/print gamut. A symmetric wheel therefore hides important asymmetries.

## Character-first palettes

He favors organizing palettes by **character** — combinations of lightness and chroma such as pale, muted, deep, vivid, dark, or middle-ranging — rather than making hue relation the primary classifier. He points to Ellen Divers's work and to Shigenobu Kobayashi as useful precedents.

The practical claim is that judgments such as calm versus energetic or relaxed versus intense track these lightness/chroma structures more reliably than simple labels such as “blue is calm” or “red is energetic.” Hue remains available for communication and association, but it is not the structural engine of harmony.

## Legibility

A palette can have coherent character and still fail functionally if its elements have insufficient lightness contrast. Donahue therefore treats lightness separation as a separate design requirement rather than assuming a harmonious hue relation guarantees readable structure.

## Albers connection

This pushes directly toward studying **relations among actual appearances** rather than nominal hue categories. For our experiments, a two-color or multi-color state should record lightness/chroma structure and contextual appearance instead of reducing the relation to an angle around a wheel.
