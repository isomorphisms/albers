## Demonstration

Donahue shows a 1975 Frans Gerritsen color diagram built from transparent overlays. Rather than presenting a finished wheel as an unexplained icon, the pages let the viewer physically assemble the relationship between spectral absorption and trichromatic perception.

## Claim ledger

1. Three marked sensitivity regions stand in for the three broad human cone-response channels.
2. A yellow colorant is represented as preferentially removing short-wavelength light while allowing more middle and long wavelengths through.
3. A magenta colorant is represented as removing a middle band.
4. A cyan colorant is represented as removing longer wavelengths.
5. The transparent pages are overlaid progressively so that the viewer can see how combinations of absorptions produce familiar subtractive mixtures.
6. In the yellow-plus-magenta example, short and middle portions are suppressed enough that the remaining long-wavelength-biased signal appears red.
7. Adding the cyan layer completes the construction and gives a whole hue-circle picture in which different hue appearances correspond to different spectral balances reaching the visual system.
8. Donahue values the diagram because it connects pigment/filter behavior to the observer instead of teaching CMY as three arbitrary magic words.

## What the model is doing

The wheel is simultaneously about two layers that classroom diagrams often separate too sharply:

- physical filtering/absorption changes the spectrum reaching the eye;
- the visual system compresses that spectrum into a small number of receptor responses.

The overlay is therefore not merely a mixture chart. It is an explanatory bridge between stimulus and perception.

## Technical qualification

The broad “red/green/blue sensitivity regions” are teaching symbols, not narrow independent wavelength bins, and real pigment reflectance/transmittance curves are not ideal rectangular filters. Preserve Gerritsen/Donahue's explanatory construction without mistaking it for a complete spectral model.

## Albers relevance

This is useful for keeping two distinct causes of similar-looking patches separate. Two samples may produce similar cone responses while having different spectra, and those differences can reappear under changed illumination or reproduction. An Albers archive therefore should preserve both appearance coordinates and, where available, material/spectral provenance.
