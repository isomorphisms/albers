## Structure of the lecture

Donahue begins by treating the conventional twelve-hue wheel as a map that should be tested rather than trusted. When sampled wheel colors are plotted in a CIE-derived space, their perceptual separations are badly uneven: some neighboring labels are almost coincident while large regions, especially through greens/cyans, are compressed. Printing gamut also limits what the paper wheel can show.

He then plots actual paints. The important shift is from abstract hue names to particular pigments. Cadmium red, cobalt blue, and a primary yellow do not occupy an ideal equilateral geometry, so equal-looking changes in paint ratio do not produce equal hue changes.

A blue/yellow mixing demonstration makes the nonlinearity visible. Ratios on opposite sides of 1:1 need not be symmetrically spaced in appearance. Tinting strength matters: a physically small quantity of a strong pigment can dominate a much larger quantity of a weaker one.

## Chroma and spectral behavior

Donahue distinguishes maximum chroma available to different hue/pigment regions rather than pretending every spoke of a wheel reaches an equivalent maximum. His red, yellow, and blue examples have different reachable chroma.

The classic disappointing purple from cadmium red plus cobalt blue is explained materially: their reflectance profiles leave little shared spectral energy in the mixture, so the result loses chroma. A magenta pigment with a more suitable spectral profile can produce a much more vivid purple.

## White, black, and neutrals

Adding white is not a pure lightness operation. It can also alter hue and reduce chroma. Similarly, black plus yellow can move toward olive/green rather than producing a simple darker yellow. Neutralization therefore cannot be modeled as movement along one independent slider.

## General lesson

A paint name is not enough information to predict a mixture. Pigment identity, spectral behavior, tinting strength, proportions, medium, and gamut all matter. Donahue’s Color Disc is presented as a better practical map because actual pigments are plotted in measured positions rather than forced into ideal RYB wedges.

## Albers connection

This is the material/reproduction half of the problem: an Albers swatch is not merely an abstract Lab point. If we later reproduce exercises with real pigments or printed samples, the path used to produce a nominal target color can matter, including metamerism and gamut limitations.
