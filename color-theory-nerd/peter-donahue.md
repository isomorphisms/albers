# Peter T. Donahue / Color Nerd

## Who he is

Peter T. Donahue is an artist, painter, graphic designer, art teacher, and color-theory educator. His public work sits in an unusually useful middle ground between studio practice, history of color systems, perception science, and practical pedagogy.

His own site describes the project succinctly: he is an artist and color-theory educator whose teaching leans heavily on **perception science** and **color literacy**. He publishes as **Color Nerd** on YouTube, runs workshops, and gives talks on subjects including design, medieval color theory, and paint mixing.

The Colour Literacy Project describes him as a painter, graphic designer, and educator known for lessons on:

- color perception;
- light mixing;
- pigment mixing;
- color harmony;
- the history and teaching of color theory.

He teaches art full time at Morristown-Beard School in New Jersey. Drawing America also describes substantial lecture, workshop, consulting, and studio-painting work.

## Why he is relevant to an Albers project

Donahue repeatedly asks a very Albers-like question: **what does a color actually do in perception, rather than what name or rule have we inherited for it?**

But his route is different. Albers typically begins with carefully staged visual experience and delays theory. Donahue often begins from an inherited rule — for example RYB primaries, geometric complements, warm/cool labels, or a flat color wheel — and asks whether it survives contact with:

1. actual perception;
2. actual pigment mixtures;
3. spectra and physical light;
4. modern perceptual color spaces;
5. the historical record.

That makes his material a useful modern counterpoint to *Interaction of Color*.

## Recurring intellectual commitments

### 1. A color wheel is a map, and maps can lie

A wheel that spaces hues evenly on paper can badly misrepresent perceptual distance, achievable pigment mixtures, or gamut. Donahue treats the familiar artist's RYB wheel as one historical convention, not a law of nature.

His preferred teaching tools try to preserve more of the geometry of perceived color and real mixing behavior. His Color Disc and later tools use perceptual organization rather than assuming that twelve named wedges are equally separated in any useful sense.

### 2. Do not make beginners learn a false rule merely because it is simple

A repeated pedagogical theme is that a simple model is not actually simple if students later have to unlearn it. He is especially impatient with teaching "red + blue = purple" or geometric complement rules as if all paints were ideal abstract colors.

The alternative is not maximal technicality. He tries to find a model simple enough to teach but honest enough to fail gracefully.

### 3. Paints are physical substances, not abstract color coordinates

His paint-mixing lectures emphasize pigment spectra, tinting strength, opacity/transparency, and nonlinear mixing. Two paints with names that seem theoretically complementary can produce an unexpectedly dull mixture because their spectral reflectance curves leave little light to survive the mixture.

Conversely, a pigment such as quinacridone magenta can produce purples that a cadmium-red/cobalt-blue pair cannot, not because the word "magenta" has magical status but because the physical pigment has a different spectral profile.

### 4. Perceptual dimensions need precise names

Donahue spends substantial effort separating concepts that ordinary color software and ordinary art instruction blur together:

- hue;
- lightness;
- brightness;
- chroma;
- saturation;
- colorfulness;
- luminance;
- temperature.

A characteristic example is his distinction between **chroma** (difference from a neutral of comparable lightness) and **saturation** (colorfulness relative to brightness). His visualizations make the distinction spatial rather than merely verbal.

### 5. Color is three-dimensional before it is a wheel

Flat hue circles are useful projections, but they conceal lightness and chroma. Donahue frequently uses hue planes, Munsell-like solids, CIE-derived spaces, gamut boundaries, and other three-dimensional models to show why equal angular steps on a wheel are insufficient.

### 6. Additive and subtractive color are related, not separate folklore systems

He uses RGB lights and CMY shadows to show the physical complement relation directly. In his longer mixing lecture, pigment mixing is presented as more complicated than the schoolbook phrase "subtractive mixing": real paint behavior combines spectral subtraction with effects that make mixture paths curve through perceptual space.

He has used Harold Küppers's phrase **integrated mixing**, David Briggs's work, CIECAM02 hue angles, and Kubelka-Munk theory as reference points while deliberately searching for simpler artist-facing explanations.

### 7. History matters because bad diagrams have descendants

A large part of the channel is historical detective work. Donahue tracks the provenance of wheels and teaching systems: Moses Harris, Ostwald, Munsell, Gerritsen, old industrial/educational selectors, obscure women color theorists, and Bauhaus-era pedagogy.

The point is usually not antiquarian trivia. He wants to know *why* a particular diagram became normal, what problem it originally solved, and whether later teachers mistook that contingent solution for universal theory.

## Painter rather than merely commentator

The recollection that he mixes his own colors is substantially right.

His university paint-mixing lecture includes live acrylic mixing. His Drawing America courses require artist-grade pigments and build exercises around actual mixtures. The Color Temperature for Painters course is demonstrated in oils and uses palette preparation to model golden-hour light, atmospheric perspective, and mixed-temperature illumination. The Famous Palettes course pairs historical lectures with painting demonstrations of palettes associated with Caravaggio, Turner, Mary Cassatt, and Munsell.

This matters because his criticism of traditional color teaching is not purely abstract. He repeatedly asks whether the model helps a painter predict what will happen on a palette.

## How to read him critically

Donahue is most useful here as a **practitioner-educator and research synthesizer**. A short video about bird tetrachromacy or color temperature is a pedagogical model, not a substitute for the primary vision-science literature. Likewise, a historical short often compresses a complicated genealogy into a sharp argument.

For this repository, the productive rule is:

> Preserve the visual insight and the historical question, then follow the references when the exact scientific or historical claim matters.

That is also a good way to read Albers.
