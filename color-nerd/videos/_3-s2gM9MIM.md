## Claim ledger

1. Donahue opens Wilhelm Ostwald's *Color Primer* and identifies the 24-hue circle as the object under review.
2. He immediately separates its purpose from a pigment recipe: this is not an RYB mixing wheel but a psychological/perceptual ordering system.
3. The placement of a lemon-like yellow opposite an ultramarine-like blue illustrates that the wheel's oppositions follow Ostwald's organization rather than a later classroom convention.
4. Donahue notes some historically contingent terminology, including Germanized naming choices made in the World War I period.
5. He traces the system's spread through English-language translations and teaching materials connected with firms such as Winsor & Newton and Reeves.
6. He points to adoption in British school materials during the 1930s and to Egbert Jacobson as an important American promoter in the 1930s–40s.
7. He shows that Ostwald-derived standard colors remained commercially visible later, including mid-century ink offerings.
8. He then asks why the system receded. His answer is not simply that the wheel was “wrong”: it functioned as a closed standard and became awkward when new materials exceeded or failed to fit the predefined slots.
9. The historical lesson is that a color system can be successful for decades and still lose utility when its standardization scheme cannot adapt to new gamuts/materials.

## Series method visible here

This first Color Wheel Wednesday establishes a recurring procedure:

- identify the artifact and date;
- determine the problem it was intended to solve;
- distinguish ordering, mixing, perception, and commerce;
- trace adoption through institutions/materials;
- explain failure or survival historically rather than scoring the wheel against one timeless template.
